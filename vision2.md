# IMPULSE: VISION DOCUMENT

## Part I: The Fantasy

### The Silence and the Flash
Space warfare is not a dogfight. It is a mathematical inevitability.

For three days, you have been drifting dark behind the shadow of Io. Your reactors are throttled down to a magnetic whisper. Your heat sinks are nearing saturation, holding onto every joule of waste heat to keep your infrared signature indistinguishable from the background radiation.

You are not waiting for a fight. You are waiting for a trajectory.

On the tactical plot, the enemy convoy is a confident blue line intersecting the void. They are fusion-driven, heavy, and loud. They believe they own the vacuum. They believe their point-defense grids are impenetrable. And at zero relative velocity, they might be right.

But you aren't at zero relative velocity. You are a coiled spring.

### The Rhythm of the Void
The simulation floor is 60x real-time because the void is too large for human patience. You watch hours compress into minutes. You watch the geometry of the engagement lock into place.

This is the majority of your command: **The Approach.** It is a contemplative, quiet pressure. You check your firing solutions. You adjust the formation spacing by fractions of a degree. You look at the "Rules of Engagement" you set three hours ago—Silent Running, Hold Fire, Sprint-and-Drift—and you trust them.

Then, the intercept.

### The Joust
You break cover. You light the drives.

On the enemy sensors, you appear not as a ship, but as a sudden, impossible heat spike. You are moving at them with a closure rate of 40 kilometers per second. There is no time for them to turn. There is no time to bring their heavy broadsides to bear.

This is where the game shifts. The long, hypnotic minutes of the approach shatter.

You drop the simulation to **1x—Real Time.** In most games, this would be the pause button. Here, it is simply the moment you hold your breath.

At 40km/s, the terminal engagement range of 1,000km vanishes in twenty-five seconds.

You don't tap buttons to fire guns. You don't joystick a starship. You are the Admiral, and the orders were given long ago. You watch the "Alpha Strike" doctrine execute.

Your ships dump their capacitors. Railguns fire slugs that add their own velocity to the ships' massive kinetic energy. Missiles detach and sprint, maneuvering too hard for human pilots to survive.

It is a joust. Two vectors intersecting in the dark.

And then, silence.

## Part II: The Mechanics

### 1. The Asymmetry of Physics
The Inner System has industrial output. You have better physics. The Rebellion is built on two specific technological breakthroughs that define the gameplay loop.

**Strategic Advantage: Advanced Radiators (The Long Game)**
Most travel is done via Fusion drives, used by both sides. The limiter on fusion isn't fuel; it's heat. The Inner System ships are massive because they require enormous, heavy cooling towers to keep their crews from cooking.
* **The Mechanic:** Your breakthrough in thermal dissipation allows your ships to be lighter. Lighter mass means higher acceleration for the same energy input.
* **The Result:** You dictate the engagement. You can sustain burns longer than they can. You can outrun their patrols on the strategic map, choose where to fight, and retreat before they can bring their heavy fleets to bear.

**Tactical Advantage: Antimatter (The Short Game)**
Antimatter is rare, volatile, and precious. You don't use it to commute; you use it to kill.
* **The Mechanic:** Antimatter drives offer unparalleled Thrust-to-Weight ratios and Specific Impulse (ISP), scaling incredibly well on small masses (corvettes, strike craft, missiles).
* **The Result:** In the tactical "Joust," your strike craft can pull G-forces that would tear a fusion ship apart. You burn this precious resource to execute maneuvers impossible for the enemy—last-second dodges, high-G intercepts, and terminal velocity sprints.

### 2. The Engineering Floor
"Plan, Don't React" begins in the hangar. You do not just build ships; you design the systems that dictate their behavior.

**Variable Reaction Mass**
Every engine can be tuned. Do you inject more mass into the exhaust stream?
* **High Mass Flow:** Increases Thrust (Acceleration), but destroys your ISP (Fuel Efficiency). Great for combat sprints.
* **Low Mass Flow:** High ISP, low Thrust. Essential for long-haul strategic maneuvers.
* **Gameplay:** You must balance your hold space between Reaction Mass (water/hydrogen) and Mission Payload. A ship designed for a long patrol cannot maneuver hard without running dry.

**Ordnance Design**
You design the missiles.
* **The Chassis:** Long-range cruise bus or high-G interceptor?
* **The Payload:** Nuclear pump-laser, kinetic penetrator, or electronic warfare jammer?
* **The AI:** This is critical. You program the missile's terminal behavior. Does it weave to avoid PD? Does it target the hottest engine plume or the active radar source? A poorly designed missile is just expensive space junk.

### 3. The Time Interface
The simulation floor is 60x. Real-time (1x) is a luxury—and at these distances, it *is* slow-motion.

You have a limited pool of **Focus**. Spend it to drop to 1x. This is your bullet-time, and it is precious.

Focus is not abstract. It is morale. It is the steadiness of your command staff, the clarity of your tactical officers, the will of a fleet that believes it can win. Victories replenish it. Losses drain it. A fleet that is losing has less capacity to think clearly—exactly when they need it most.

* **100,000 km (The Setup):** At 60x, you are the Admiral in the long silence. You watch vectors evolve, manage heat buildup, position your fleet relative to orbital bodies. You trust your doctrine. You wait.
* **10,000 km (The Commit):** You spend Focus to drop to 1x. Sixteen minutes of missile flight becomes sixteen real seconds at 60x—but at 1x, you have time to breathe. You verify doctrine. You update priorities. You cannot micro the ships, but you can adjust the plan.
* **1,000 km (The Merge):** If you have Focus left, spend it now. At 1x, the terminal engagement unfolds in real seconds—enough to watch, to understand, to feel the weight of decisions already made. If you're out of Focus, you watch at 60x: a blur of sensor ghosts and PD tracers, the outcome of your preparation revealed in moments. There is no pause button. There is no save-scumming. Your doctrine either works or it doesn't.

The rhythm emerges: long stretches of compressed time where you trust your automation, punctuated by intense moments where you spend Focus to *be present* for what matters. A veteran knows when to spend. A novice spends constantly and runs dry at the worst moment.

### 4. Logistics as Gameplay
The Rebellion runs on scarcity.

* **Heat:** Your primary tactical resource. You start engagements "Cold" (heatsinks empty). Every shot, every burn fills the sink. When you hit 100%, you must open radiators (lighting up like a flare) or suffer internal damage.
* **Antimatter:** The strategic currency. Gathered from raids on specific containment facilities. Used only for high-stakes combat maneuvers.
* **Reaction Mass:** The operational tether. You can have the best ships in the system, but if you burn too hard on the approach, you will be adrift—a sitting duck for their fusion-drive battleships to catch and crush.

### 5. The Core Loop
1.  **Design:** Build the ships, missiles, and doctrines to solve a specific tactical problem (e.g., "Crack a heavy freighter convoy").
2.  **Intercept:** Use your radiator advantage to catch them on the strategic map.
3.  **Approach:** Use 60x time to drift into position, managing heat to stay hidden.
4.  **Joust:** Drop to 1x. Burn Antimatter. Watch your design execute the plan in a few seconds of violence.
5.  **Survive:** Assess damage, manage the heat spike from the battle, and limp back to the dark before reinforcements arrive.

### 6. The Topography of the Void
Space is not featureless. It has geography.

**Lagrange Points**
The gravitational sweet spots where the pull of two bodies balances. Stable positions. Natural waypoints. Stations cluster here because station-keeping is cheap. Control a Lagrange point and you control the junction.

**Manifolds**
The low-energy pathways threading between Lagrange points—the chaotic corridors where a nudge sends you coasting for months on minimal fuel. Bulk freighters use these. They are slow, predictable, and efficient.

They are also the arteries of commerce. And arteries can be cut.

* **The Mechanic:** Manifolds and Lagrange points create chokepoints in a domain that should have none. They are where patrols concentrate, where ambushes wait, where battles have *reasons* to happen in specific places.
* **The Result:** Strategic terrain. You don't just chase enemy ships—you control positions, interdict routes, force them to defend everywhere while you strike where they are weak.

### 7. The Enemy
The Inner System is not scrappy. It is *institutional*.

Earth and Mars have populations in the billions. Industrial capacity that dwarfs the outer system by orders of magnitude. Fleets so large that losing a dozen cruisers is a rounding error.

They do not need to outmaneuver you. They need to find you and concentrate force. Their doctrine is presence: patrol the lanes, garrison the stations, and when rebels appear, bring enough firepower that tactics become irrelevant.

This is how empires fight. They follow procedures. They reinforce attacked positions. They escalate systematically. They are a machine, not a duelist—and machines can be understood.

* **The Result:** You are playing against the map, the clock, and the weight of industrial civilization. The challenge is surviving against a force that can afford to lose and lose and lose until you make one mistake. Learn their patterns. Exploit their rigidity. Make them pay for every AU they try to control.

### 8. Victory and Survival
You cannot conquer Earth. You cannot match their production. You cannot win a war of attrition.

So what does winning look like?

**Survival is the baseline.** How long can you hold out? How many convoys can you raid, how many stations can you take, how much of the outer system can you hold before their fleets grind you down? This is the default mode: a roguelike campaign where the question is not *if* you lose, but *when* and *how gloriously*.

**But victory is possible.**

Earth has defenses. Orbital platforms, interceptor grids, early warning networks. They are formidable—but not infinite. A campaign of attrition can degrade them. A decade of raids can bleed their outer system garrisons. And antimatter, carefully hoarded, can be weaponized.

A MIRV strike. Antimatter warheads. Slipping through degraded defenses at a moment of chaos.

It is not easy. It is not likely. It requires a campaign played well across dozens of hours, resources hoarded, opportunities seized, and a final strike executed with precision.

But the kill-shot exists. And knowing it exists changes everything.
